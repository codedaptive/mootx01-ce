# Decision: The Clock Maker, the Integrity Triangle, Time, and the Hot Path

## 1. Summary

This document decides how an event's time is created, sealed, ordered, read, and merged across the stack — SubstrateLib, PersistenceKit, LocusKit, CorpusKit, and GLK. It builds on the audit-log-as-source-of-truth model and the already-built `AuditGate` / `VocabularyValidator`.

The thesis is one sentence. The top-ranking entity present *makes* one clock; everything beneath it *holds* that clock; "synced" is just the absence of a second clock. Every event is stamped once by that clock, the stamp is sealed into the event's identity together with its content, and state is read through a single materialized projection folded in the order the system learned things — so reads never re-fold history and the record can prove who knew what, first, with proof.

These positions are decided. Four questions that were open during design are resolved in this document (§9), and one design alternative we actively rejected — dual-clock projection — is recorded with its reasoning in §11, because the reason it failed is itself load-bearing.

Already built: the `AuditGate` write gate, the `VocabularyValidator`, and adjective bit 27 (`sealed`), both Swift and Rust legs, with conformance tests. What this document specifies and is *not* yet built: the HLC generator primitive, the seal/triangle storage, custody mode, the materialized-projection read path, and the federation wrap. No new actor is introduced; every piece lands on the existing layering.

## 2. The Clock Maker

HLC is a Hybrid Logical Clock: `(physical, logical, node)`. `physical` is wall-clock and human-readable; `logical` breaks ties and absorbs clock stalls; `node` is the final deterministic tiebreaker. The update rule on each event sets `physical = max(local wall-clock, last physical)`, and if that left `physical` unchanged it bumps `logical`, otherwise it resets `logical` to zero. Comparison is lexicographic over `(physical, logical, node)`, a total order every node agrees on. This is the ingest ordering clock, distinct from empirical time (§4).

It is a maker, not an owner. Ownership implies a standing role, and there is none — a clock is made once and then simply exists. The Clock Maker is whoever calls `open`: the top-ranking entity present. For a standalone kit that is the kit's own boot; in an estate it is GLK. `open(over: log, nodeID:)` is the making event: it seeds the generator to `max(existing-log-max, wall-clock)` and claims the maker node id. Because it seeds from the log's maximum, a restart and a promotion are the same operation — a top entity comes up, finds existing history, and continues the clock. Everyone else is a holder: holders receive the opened generator by injection at construction and call `tick()`; they never call `open`.

Placement. The `HLCGenerator` (`open`, `tick`) is a SubstrateLib primitive — pure mechanism, holdable by any layer, the identical type whether a bare kit or an estate makes it. The decision of *who* calls `open` belongs to the top entity (GLK in an estate); topology picks the maker, not the primitive. Holder wiring is initializer injection at construction boundaries: GLK opens, then hands the one instance into `LocusKit(hlc:)` and `CorpusKit(hlc:)`. The maker-node-id marker lives in the PersistenceKit header; storage holds and guards the marker, while the substrate defines what claiming it means.

The single-maker guard. `open` claims a maker field in the header. The one way this corrupts is two makers over one log — two node ids, two clocks advancing independently. The guard, decided here, is that a second `open` over a log that already has a maker is **refused**. Promotion is then an explicit, logged `takeover` operation that records the handoff as an event — never a silent second clock. (See §9 for why refuse-plus-explicit beats auto-takeover.)

Estate sync falls out for free. Because GLK is the single maker and CORPUS and LOCUS are holders of GLK's one instance, a LOCUS event and a CORPUS event carry stamps from the same sequence and the same node id; they order against each other with no coordination between the kits. Sync is structural, not an operation.

## 3. The integrity triangle

Three legs each pin a different failure, and they mutually cross-check only if the seal hashes the HLC *and* the content together — a seal over the HLC alone collapses the triangle into two unrelated locks. The plaintext HLC, produced by `tick()`, is carried as a field on the `AuditEvent` and stored by PersistenceKit as the ordering key; this is the ordering leg. The content-plus-HLC seal is computed at `AuditGate.admit` as SHA-256 over the wire fields including the full HLC with node id, folded into the event id; this is the binding leg, and it breaks if anyone moves an event in the order or swaps content under a stamp. The companion HLC seal stores the sealed HLC as a companion to the plaintext clock value, so timestamp tampering is detectable as its own fault class without re-deriving the whole event id; this is the clock-integrity leg. Tamper with any corner and the other two disagree.

The seal must cover the maker node id, not only `physical` and `logical`, because "who knew first" is provable only if the origin stamp is bound to which maker produced it. This is a concrete requirement on the content-ID construction.

The triangle is per-maker, and therefore coherent across kits. Because GLK is the single maker, LOCUS's and CORPUS's events share one sequence and one node id, so a single coherent triangle spans everything beneath one maker. It is per-maker, not per-kit.

One trust boundary is named rather than assumed. The seal proves an event was not altered and binds it to a *claimed* node id; binding that claimed id to a *real* peer is identity attestation at the pairing handshake (the capability/attestation exchange of the federation decision, Appendix C), a separate mechanism. "With proof" assumes that attestation for cross-estate claims.

The seal bit is a one-bit trust hint, not the proof. Seal state is adjective bit 27 (`sealed`, polarity 1 = sealed), allocated on both legs and pinned by test. It is an orthogonal flag, not a value on the trust scale: a record carries a content trust level (verbatim through ambient, bits 18–23) and a seal state independently, so a canonical-but-not-yet-sealed thought is representable. The adjective layout after allocation is state 0–5, sensitivity 6–11, exportability 12–17, trust 18–23, state-extension 24, lineage-clustering 25, dreaming-recalc-required 26, sealed 27, reserved 28–63. Bit 27 was the first free position and it sits beside bit 26, the other dreaming-pass obligation flag, which is coherent because sealing is itself a dreaming obligation. One bit was chosen for compute: it joins with any other adjective query at register speed in the same integer reads already load, no extra load and no branch, at popcount cost. The bit is a cached assertion that a valid seal exists; it is not the proof, which lives elsewhere, and if the bit ever disagrees with the seal the seal wins. The kind of seal — lazy-consolidated versus strict-contemporaneous, and any attestation identity — rides with the proof, not in the bitmap; the bit says only sealed yes or no. The bit doubles as the consolidation work-queue, since unsealed records are exactly those where `sealed` is false.

The bit is mode-stamped, never computed. The write stamps the bit as a constant from the custody mode — strict writes 1, lazy writes 0 — a branchless mode lookup with no calculation and no hash on the write path. Strict touches the bit once, writing 1 at the write because the seal mints in the same step; lazy touches it twice, writing 0 at the write and letting the dream pass flip it to 1 when the seal is computed, each touch still just a constant write. The only thing that ever earns the 1 is the seal existing, and that earning happens off the hot path. The bit must be set only by the code that writes or verifies the seal, never independently, or the hint would lie.

## 4. Three time concepts, kept distinct

The federation and backlog cases force three timestamps apart, and conflating any two loses information that cannot be reconstructed. The empirical date is when the thing actually happened in the world — a photo taken in 2015 — content-derived, about the material, belonging to no clock; this is `eventTime`, sealed at origin as content and riding along on ingest, never re-stamped. The origin HLC is when a system first recorded it, the origin maker's gate stamp, the "who knew it first" proof, kept verbatim. The ingest HLC is when your system recorded receipt, your maker's stamp, which orders it in your timeline.

The correction this encodes: a federated item's clock is its origin HLC, the proof of the other party's first-knowledge — not its empirical date. The empirical date is a third thing inside the content. Empirical date and origin HLC must not be merged into a single "authored date" field.

## 5. The hot path — speed, spend, storage

Speed goes to reads. A naive audit read is O(history): to answer "what is it now," it folds every event for the row. Reads run constantly — every query, recall filter, and UI surface — and a naive read gets slower as history grows. That is the hot path.

Spend goes onto writes. The system keeps a materialized projection, a current snapshot per live row, beside the log. Reads hit the snapshot in constant time. Writes append the sealed event and fold it forward into the snapshot, moving the fold cost onto the write, which is rare and latency-tolerant. This shape already exists: the live `drawers` row beside `bitmap_audit` is a log-plus-materialized-state split, and the LocusKit migration swaps the log half to sealed snapshot events while keeping the current-state row as the read target.

Storage economy is a log question, because the snapshot is bounded at one row per live record while the log is unbounded across every event ever. Two levers apply. The per-event seal and companion-hash overhead is small, fixed, and non-negotiable — it is the triangle. Checkpointing writes a sealed checkpoint at a fixed interval (decided: every 1000 events, configurable per moot; see §9) so a cold rebuild replays only from the last checkpoint and ancient events can age to cold storage without losing verifiability or rebuildability. Fold-forward is the per-event step; the checkpoint is the periodic one, and they are different mechanisms for different purposes (current state versus bounded recovery).

Cost concentrates at out-of-order ingest, not at "federation" as such. In-order local writes fold forward cheaply. The expensive write is a bulk arrival of events whose meaningful order is empirical, landing out of empirical order and forcing a re-fold of affected rows. The ten-year-backlog ingest and the federation merge are the same case: events with old origin and empirical dates, ingested now. Ordered by ingest HLC they are in-order, all recent, and cheap; only when queried by empirical date would they insert into the middle of a long timeline — and §11 records that empirical-order folding of *state* is rejected, so that re-fold cost does not arise as a maintained projection. The federation merge is also where nested-triangle verification runs, so cost and verification concentrate at the same boundary.

## 6. Federation and backlog — verbatim, nested triangles

Verbatim carry is mandatory because the origin stamp is the proof of who-knew-first; re-sealing an incoming event under the receiver's maker would erase the evidence that the origin knew it first. An incoming event is therefore not re-stamped but wrapped. The origin event is carried verbatim — its origin plaintext HLC, its origin seal, its origin content — so its triangle stays intact and verifies against the origin maker. The receiver stamps its own ingest HLC around it and seals the fact that it received this event-id at this time, and that is the receiver's outer triangle. Two intact triangles, nested, neither rewritten. The origin HLC proves first-knowledge; the receive HLC orders it locally; and a receiver's log legitimately holds multiple makers' seals, which is the normal multi-maker situation across time, not corruption.

Backlog is the same structure as federation. A permanently ingested decade-spanning backlog has origin and empirical dates far in the past and a recent ingest HLC: same wrap, same nesting.

## 7. Custody mode — strict versus lazy, over one invariant seal

Seal state is a trust level, and the cost and timing of sealing is a custody choice set per moot at creation, over one byte-identical seal construction. The axis is not secure versus casual; it is whether a record is a chain-of-custody record or a personal-integrity record — whether it must prove something, later, to someone who does not trust the author.

A strict moot — research notes, a priority claim, "I had the idea first" — mints the seal and the HLC atomically at the write, contemporaneously. That contemporaneity is the evidence, so deferral is forbidden: a seal minted later nullifies the priority claim, because the proof's time no longer coincides with the moment of origin. The write stamps the seal bit 1. A lazy moot — personal life, no adversary, "I am not using my moot as my alibi" — does the fast path only at the write and defers the hash to dreaming; the seal's only job there is honest-fault detection, which is time-insensitive. The write stamps the bit 0 and consolidation flips it to 1. The two modes answer two different evidentiary questions, contemporaneous priority versus content-intact, which is why custody is a per-moot setting rather than global. Both produce the same seal; strict versus lazy is purely when it is computed, never what it is.

The irreversibility is the anti-backdating guarantee. A lazy moot can upgrade to strict going forward, but its past lazy-sealed events can never retroactively become contemporaneous, because their seals were minted at dream-time, not write-time. That is a feature: it is exactly the backdating the system exists to prevent.

The unsealed window is the emergent tier, not a hole. An unsealed record is at once low custody-trust and high recency — the same physical fact, that the seal has not run, seen from two directions. It is the freshly-captured, not-yet-consolidated thought. Sealing is consolidation, and trust rises as a record moves from emergent-unsealed to consolidated-sealed; a strict moot consolidates immediately, a lazy moot lets thought sit in the emergent tier and consolidates during dreaming. The boundary rule holds throughout: an event may cross a trust boundary — federate, export, be audited, be relied on by another authority — only if already sealed, and only a strict seal carries a priority claim.

On an iPhone-only deployment this resolves cleanly. In lazy mode writes are cryptographically free and instant — tick, gate, fold, stamp the bit 0, append — and the dream and seal pass is idle-and-power-gated, which is exactly the deferrable-batch window iOS grants background compute on its own terms of charging, screen-off, and thermals. The unsealed window can be long, hours or a day, which is fine because honest-fault detection is latency-insensitive. The dream pass must be resumable and incremental, since the OS will yank the background window mid-pass; it runs in HLC order, flips bits as it goes, and resumes from the first still-unsealed record next window, and because the seal bit is itself the work-queue the queue survives interruption with no separate checkpoint state. Strict mode is fully supported on the same device, simply slower per write because it pays the SHA-256 at the write, in the manner of a Bitcoin wallet signing a transaction: the felt latency is the contemporaneous notarization, on a deliberate act, not a drag on every interaction. The device never blocks strict; it makes the cost honest and felt, which for a custody record is correct. Strict and lazy moots coexist on one device the way a wallet's heavy signing coexists with otherwise instant apps.

## 8. PersistenceKit's role — declare and enforce, never author

A kit declares integrity policy on the table at build time, as a schema property alongside `appendOnly`: HLC-required or not, sealed or not, encrypted or not. PersistenceKit enforces that policy — it refuses a write missing a required HLC or seal, verifies seals on read, keys on `(eventID, hlc)`, and arms append-only — but it does not author: it never generates the HLC or computes the seal. The HLC comes from the one maker upstream and the seal is computed at the gate. The operative word is enforce, not handle, because the moment storage authors the clock the triangle stops composing across kits.

This makes read-only verifiable rather than merely promised. Append-only plus the seal upgrades immutable history from a promise to a property that is detectable if violated, which matters most for the in-memory backend, where no engine enforces immutability for you. A genuinely read-only instance is also expressible: open with the gate never armed for writes, the HLC loaded to its last value but not advancing, and append refused, yielding a verifiable read-only replica that can project state and verify every seal but structurally cannot mint an event — useful as a federation or audit-verification sandbox.

## 9. Resolved questions

These four were open during design and are decided here.

The promotion mechanism is refuse-plus-explicit-takeover, not auto-takeover. Auto-takeover would make a second `open` silently become the clock maker, which is the precise "two makers, silent" failure the single-maker guard exists to prevent; making it automatic defeats the guard. A takeover is a change of custody authority and must be a deliberate, logged act, so `open` refuses on an already-owned log and a separate `takeover` operation records the handoff as an event.

The projection is folded in ingest-HLC order only; dual-clock projection is rejected (the reasoning is in §11). There is one projection axis.

The checkpoint cadence is a constant interval, defaulting to 1000 events, configurable per moot. Adaptive-to-churn is an optimization with no present evidence it is needed, and a constant is correct, predictable, and tunable later with no schema change.

The companion seal is stored as a column on the event row, not a sibling table. The seal is one-to-one with the event, written and read with it, and never queried independently of it; a sibling table would add a join for data that always travels together, so same-row locality is the right shape and the one the hot path wants.

## 10. Layer summary

SubstrateLib owns the primitives and authors the values: the HLC generator, the gate, the seal (SHA-256), and the bitfield operations. PersistenceKit owns the storage contracts, the header and maker-marker, the table integrity flags, append-only, and `(eventID, hlc)` keying; it declares-and-enforces and stores, and never authors. LocusKit and CorpusKit own row mutations through the gate, the materialized projection, and their vocabulary union; they are holders of the clock. GLK owns the making (`open`), holder injection, the estate-wide single maker, and the federation wrap; in an estate GLK is the Clock Maker.

## 11. Rejected components and why

This project's discipline is that a component must justify its own existence: prove the system cannot do without it, or remove it. One designed component failed that test and is recorded here so the rejection is not silently re-litigated.

**Dual-clock projection — rejected.** The proposal at one stage was that a row's state be foldable by either clock: by ingest time ("what did the system know on date X") and by empirical time ("what was true in the world on date Y"), as two maintained projections. Applying the test, we tried to remove the empirical-order projection and asked what breaks. Nothing does. The empirical date is already stored as sealed content on every event, so removing empirical-*order projection* removes a fold mode, not data. Every real use of the empirical date is a filter-and-sort on that field — "show me events about things that happened in 2015," or "prove this note describes a 2015 event" — none of which is a fold; you read events and sort or verify against the field. The one operation that would genuinely require folding state in empirical order — "what did the row's state look like as of empirical date Y" — is almost always incoherent, because a row's state (sensitivity, trust, lifecycle) evolved in ingest time: it was promoted, contested, and sealed in the order the system learned things, not in the order the world produced them. Asking for a trust level "as of" a date before the system had even ingested the row is undefined. Empirical order therefore does not survive as a projection axis: the empirical date is a content attribute to filter and sort on, never a fold axis for state. State has exactly one legitimate order, ingest-HLC, which is the order the chain of custody is in. Keeping a second maintained projection would have added a fold-forward cost and a cold re-fold path to serve a question that is either answerable without it (filter/sort) or undefined (state as-of empirical). Removed.

The consequence is a simplification, not a loss: the LocusKit projection switch becomes unambiguous — `AuditLogFold` over `eventsForRow` in HLC order, one ordering, no parameterization, no second cold path — and the "fork" that appeared to block the migration dissolves, because there was only ever one fold.

A second rejection is recorded for the same reason. **Splitting the codebase by custody mode — rejected.** Holding strict mode out of the open code while shipping lazy was considered as an open-core boundary and rejected: strict and lazy are one scheduling flag over one seal, so cleaving the code along that flag protects a moat that does not yet exist and creates an artificial seam that rots into two nearly-identical paths. The seal construction, HLC, triangle, trust bit, and wire format must be open or the lazy edition and all verification cannot work, and holding back the few lines of synchronous-seal code protects little, since anyone reading the open spec reimplements it quickly — and unattested look-alike "strict" seals would actively poison the evidentiary value of real ones. The defensible moat is attestation — a trusted authority vouching to a third party that a seal was minted contemporaneously under attested conditions, which is identity- and hardware-rooted (keys, Secure Enclave, a timestamp authority) rather than algorithm-rooted — but that is an ecosystem, not a feature, and has value only downstream of shipping, once records exist and a relying party needs vouching. The decision is therefore to ship feature-complete, keep the substrate the clean open mechanism, and let any attestation business layer stack entirely above it later with no substrate surgery. The single design tax paid now to keep that option open is that the seal carries a "who attested this" authority-identity field, self-attested for the present. If a monetization idea ever requires changing the seal or the gate, that is a smell.

## 12. What this does not change

The audit log remains the source of truth and the projection is derived. The already-built `AuditGate`, `VocabularyValidator`, and the basis-plus-union model stand unchanged. This document adds the clock primitive, the seal and triangle storage, the seal trust-bit (already allocated), custody mode, the three-time model, the materialized-projection read path, and the federation wrap — all on the existing layering, with no new actor. The substrate stays the clean open mechanism, and the future, optional attestation business layer sits entirely above it.
